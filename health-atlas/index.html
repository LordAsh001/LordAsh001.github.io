    Nigeria Health Atlas

    How far is the nearest hospital, and how would an ambulance get you there?

    This atlas puts every health facility in Nigeria's national register on one map: about 70,000 clinics, health centres and hospitals. It then asks a simple question for every community: how far away is the nearest public hospital that can treat a serious emergency? The answer shows where an ambulance service would save the most lives.

    This is a research tool, not an emergency service. In an emergency, call 112.

    How an ambulance call would work

    The plan in this atlas follows systems that already work in Lagos, India, Rwanda, the UK and France.

    1. Call 112One free number, on every mobile network, any time.
    2. A trained call-taker answersThey ask what happened and decide how urgent it is.
    3. The nearest ambulance is sentFrom a station at a local health facility, aiming to arrive within about 20 minutes.
    4. Care starts on the wayTrained crew treat the patient and warn the hospital ahead.
    5. The right hospital is readyTreatment first, payment later from the national emergency fund.

    A plan for ambulances in every state

    Nigeria already has a law and a fund for emergency transport, but most patients still reach hospital by bus, car or on foot. This page uses the facility map to work out how many ambulance stations Nigeria needs and where, and what other countries teach us.

    Where the system stands

    • The law is there. The National Health Act 2014 sets aside 5% of the Basic Health Care Provision Fund for emergency medical treatment, run by a committee of the National Council on Health (s. 11(3)). The National Emergency Medical Service and Ambulance System (NEMSAS) is the vehicle.
    • Structures exist on paper in most states. NEMSAS reports EMS structures in 33 states and the FCT, with 21 states operational, as of March 2026 (Premium Times). NEMSAS itself says "we cannot say we have an equitable distribution or enough ambulances" (The Guardian).
    • Few patients use it. An industry report for Q1 2026 estimates that public ambulances carry 7% of emergency cases; 47% arrive by commercial bus and 31% by private car or on foot (Salvus Emergency, via The Guardian).
    • Few people know the number. NEMSAS reports low awareness of 112 and uneven connection across mobile networks (Premium Times).
    • One state shows it can work. Lagos State Ambulance Service (LASAMBUS) answered 44,152 of 45,277 calls (97.5%) in 2021–2025 from 26 ambulance points and four bases (Nairametrics).

    How many stations, and where

    Pick how close an ambulance should be to every community. A closer target means faster help, but more stations. Each station is placed at an existing public health facility. The computer adds stations one at a time, always choosing the spot that reaches the most people not yet covered, until 95 in every 100 Nigerians are within reach.

    Travel times are estimates: roads are taken as 1.4 times the straight-line distance, at an average of 40 km/h.

      Sources

        Method

        1. Facilities. All 70,235 records of GRID3 NGA Health Facilities v4.0 (CIESIN, Columbia University; integrates NHFR 2024/2026, NPHCDA and GRID3 v2 inputs). The copy used here was checked against the live GRID3 service by state and level of care on 7 November 2026. 58,367 records have coordinates inside Nigeria and are mapped.
        2. Referral hospital. A hospital-type facility recorded as secondary or tertiary, or typed General, Specialist, Cottage, Federal Medical Centre or Teaching/Tertiary hospital, and not recorded as closed or non-functional. Clinics, health centres, maternity homes and laboratories are left out even where the register gives them a higher level. Public referral hospital: the same, owned by federal, state or local government or the military. Tertiary: tertiary level, Federal Medical Centre or teaching hospital.
        3. Population. WorldPop 2025 population summed to 9,307 GRID3 operational wards (as published by Kemabonta, 2026). Each ward's population is placed at the median position of its mapped facilities, or at its LGA's median where the ward has none (273 wards). Wards average about 100 km², so this puts people near the main settlement of each ward.
        4. Distance. Great-circle distance from each ward point to the nearest facility of each class. Bands are reported in straight-line kilometres. For time, roads are taken as 1.4× the straight line and ambulances average 40 km/h (2.1 minutes per straight-line km).
        5. Stations. Candidate sites are the 41,402 mapped public facilities not recorded as closed or non-functional. A greedy maximal-covering algorithm adds the site that reaches the most unreached people within the radius, until 95% of the population is reached. Three radii are run: 5, 10 and 15 km.
        6. Fleet. 1 ambulance per 100,000 people is the benchmark used for India's public emergency service; 1 per 50,000 is cited by Kenya's policy institute as WHO guidance. Neither is a formal WHO standard, so both are shown as a range.

        Limitations

        • 11,706 GRID3 records (16.7%) have no coordinates, and 14,205 have no level of care. Gaps are larger in some northern states, so hospital counts there are likely too low and distances too high.
        • "Functional" status is unknown or not recorded for 49% of records; a mapped hospital may not offer 24-hour emergency care.
        • Straight-line distance understates travel in areas with few roads, rivers without bridges, or insecurity.
        • Placing ward population at facility positions makes distances look shorter than they are for scattered rural settlements.
        • GRID3 flags some records as possible duplicates (for example, Federal Medical Centre Asaba appears twice); they are kept as published.
        • The GRID3 ambulance layer (34 points in 8 states) is too incomplete to show existing ambulances.
