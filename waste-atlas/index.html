      Satellite scan

      A machine-learning model scans Sentinel-2 images of every state for patches that look like open dumps. A screening bot then ranks each candidate as likely, possible or unlikely. Candidates are not confirmed: they appear on the main map only after a person has checked them.

      Unverified model output. Quarries, building sites and bare ground can look like dumps from space.

      What the screening bot checks

      Model confidence
      How strongly the Sentinel-2 model thinks the patch is a dump.
      Persistence
      Did the same spot also look like a dump in the 2020/21 dry season? Dumps persist; building sites change.
      Burning
      NASA fire detections within 500 m from 2019 to 2025. Open burning is common at dumps.
      Shape and size
      Compact patches score higher; long thin strips are usually road verges.

      Why it matters

      You cannot manage what you have not measured. Most of Nigeria's waste ends up in open dumps that no official map records, so their size, growth and harm stay invisible to planning and budgets.

      Health

      Open dumps breed flies, mosquitoes and rats and are linked to cholera, typhoid, dysentery and malaria in nearby communities. Nigeria recorded 1,528 suspected cholera cases and 53 deaths in 30 states between January and 24 June 2024 (NCDC, cited in Isimah et al. 2026).

      Water

      Rain washes leachate from dumps into soil and groundwater. Studies around dumps in Lagos (Igando) and the FCT satellite towns found contamination in nearby wells and soils (Ige et al. 2022; Sawyerr et al. 2017).

      Climate

      Waste after use produces about 3% of global man-made greenhouse gases and 18% of man-made methane (Sun et al. 2023). Burning dumps add smoke and soot.

      Flooding

      Dumps in drains, riverbanks and wetlands block water flow and spread waste during floods, which the Water Atlas flood watch tracks for Nigeria's rivers.

      What the atlas makes possible

      • Counting and measuring: the number and area of open dumpsites in each state, updated as sites are confirmed.
      • Prioritising: sites near rivers, wells and homes can be ranked for clean-up or closure (see How it works).
      • Tracking change: satellite history shows when a site started and whether it is growing.
      • Evidence for policy: state waste agencies, researchers and NGOs can download the data and cite it.

      Sources: Isimah et al. (2026) Research Square preprint, doi:10.21203/rs.3.rs-8260552/v1; Ige et al. (2022) Applied Water Science 12:79; Sawyerr et al. (2017) J Health Pollut 7(14); Sun et al. (2023) Nature Communications 14:1444, doi:10.1038/s41467-023-37136-1.

      Stay safe

      • Stand at the edge of the site; do not walk on waste or near burning heaps.
      • Do not photograph people without their permission, including waste pickers.
      • Report what you see: the type of waste, signs of burning, and nearby water or homes.

      How it works

      Four sources feed one map. Every site carries a verification level, so you always know how sure we are.

      1. Published studies and field surveys. Dumpsites with coordinates from peer-reviewed papers and GPS surveys, each linked to its source.
      2. Satellite scan. A Random Forest model in Google Earth Engine reads a cloud-free Sentinel-2 composite (10 m) of the 2024/25 dry season, with texture, land cover (Dynamic World) and built-up density (GHSL), and finds dump-like patches of at least 0.5 ha in every state.
      3. Screening bot. Each candidate gets a 0–100 score from model confidence, persistence since 2020/21, nearby fire detections, shape and size, and is labelled likely, possible or unlikely.
      4. Human review. Reviewers check candidates and public reports on high-resolution imagery in QGIS. Only sites with clear visual evidence plus a second piece of evidence are marked confirmed and published.
      5. Community data. Landfills mapped on OpenStreetMap load live, and anyone can pin or report a site.

      Verification levels

      Limits

      • Satellite detection misses sites smaller than about 0.5 ha; reports fill that gap.
      • A site on the map may since have been closed or cleared; dates are given where known.
      • Approximate locations come from a study area or a coarse coordinate and need checking on the ground.
